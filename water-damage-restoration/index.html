Understand the water

Not all water damage is the same, and the type decides the job

CategoryTypical sourceWhat it means for cleanup
Category 1, cleanBroken supply line, tub overflow, rainwater entering from aboveDry the structure quickly, since most materials can be saved
Category 2, grayWashing machine or dishwasher discharge, sump overflowCleaning and sanitizing plus drying, with some porous materials removed
Category 3, blackSewage backup, rising groundwater or river waterContaminated, so porous materials are removed and everything is sanitized
Water that sat over timeAny category left wet for daysCan move to a higher category as bacteria and mold grow

The industry's published reference, the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration, uses these categories and describes how each is handled. A restoration company should be able to tell you which one your loss falls under and why.

Emergency

Water damage emergency: the first 48 hours

Water damage is an emergency because time works against you. Wet drywall, padding and framing begin to swell and grow mold if they stay wet, and standing water can hide electrical hazards.

Stop the water source first, then call. Our crew extracts the water, sets up drying equipment and documents the damage for your insurer.

Steps in the first hours

  • 1Stop the water source.
  • 2Keep power off in wet areas.
  • 3Move valuables to dry space.
  • 4Photograph the damage and keep damaged items.
What restoration covers

From standing water to a dry, repaired structure

Removing the water and drying

Crews extract standing water, then place air movers and dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of floors, walls and framing. Moisture meters and thermal cameras show where water has traveled behind surfaces, so drying reaches wet areas that look dry.

The equipment runs for days, and readings are taken daily. Drying stops when the materials reach their normal moisture level, not when the room feels dry.

Cleaning and repair

After drying, damaged flooring, insulation and drywall are removed if they cannot be saved, and the area is cleaned and sanitized as the category requires. Repairs follow: drywall, paint, flooring and trim.

Repair work in Oregon is usually done by a licensed contractor, so ask whether the same company does both stages or hands off the rebuild.

Time matters

Why the first day or two decides the outcome

Guidance from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency says to dry wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to limit mold growth. Wet drywall, insulation and carpet padding hold moisture and are the first to go bad. A small leak caught early can be a drying job. The same leak left for a week can become a demolition job.

Central Oregon's dry climate helps once the structure is open to dry air, but it does not rescue a wall cavity that stays closed and wet. Cold weather slows drying and can freeze water in an unheated space, so heat and airflow are managed along with the equipment.

This page does not give health advice about mold. If anyone in the home has breathing concerns, speak to a doctor, and treat visible or musty-smelling growth as something to have assessed rather than cleaned with household products.

Who fixes what

The pipe, the water and the repairs are three different jobs

A plumber fixes the cause: the split line, the failed fitting or the blocked drain. A restoration company deals with the water and the damage. Insurance usually wants documentation from both.

If a pipe froze and split, start with emergency burst pipe repair or frozen pipe repair to stop the water. If sewage came up, sewer backup explains the first steps. If the source is unclear, leak finding finds it.

Protect your claim

Documentation your insurer will want

What to do while the water is still fresh

  • 1Photograph and video every affected room, wide shots and close ups, before anything is moved.
  • 2Write down when you found the water, what you think caused it and when the source was stopped.
  • 3Keep damaged items you can store, and list each with its age and approximate value.
  • 4Save receipts for emergency repairs, equipment and temporary lodging.
  • 5Call your insurer early and ask what they require, including whether they have preferred vendors.
  • 6Do not discard wet carpet or drywall until the insurer has seen it or has told you in writing that you may.
Choosing a company

Questions to ask any restoration contractor

Before you sign

  • 1Do you hold current IICRC certifications, and may I look them up on the IICRC site?
  • 2Are you licensed with the Oregon Construction Contractors Board for the repair work?
  • 3Will you provide daily moisture readings and a drying log?
  • 4Can you show a written scope and price before work begins?
  • 5Do you work with my insurer, and who handles the billing?
  • 6Who does the repairs after drying, and is that priced separately?
