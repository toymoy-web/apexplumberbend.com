Emergency

Toilet emergency: stop an overflow right now

An overflowing toilet can soak a floor in minutes. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down, or turn the supply valve at the wall clockwise. That stops more water entering the bowl.

A leak at the base can mean a failed wax ring, and a toilet that backs up with a gurgling tub points to a deeper blockage. Our emergency plumber handles both.

The wax ring

Why leaks at the base are a bigger deal than they look

The wax ring seals the toilet outlet to the drain flange in the floor. When it fails, wastewater escapes at the base with each flush, soaks the subfloor and can rot wood before any stain appears. A faint odor or a soft spot around the toilet is a signal to act.

Replacing a wax ring means draining and lifting the toilet, cleaning the flange and setting the toilet on a new seal. If the flange itself is cracked or set too low, a repair ring or a new flange is needed.

If the subfloor is already damp, a plumber can check the extent. Leaks at the toilet can overlap with hidden leak detection when the water is traveling to a ceiling below.

Replacing a toilet

Choosing and installing a new toilet

What to buy

Measure the rough-in, the distance from the wall to the center of the drain, which is commonly 12 inches. Choose a bowl shape and height that suit the household. A WaterSense-labeled toilet uses no more than 1.28 gallons per flush and is the typical choice for new installs.

Flush performance varies more between models than the label suggests. Independent flush tests are worth a look before buying.

What installation includes

The plumber removes the old toilet, inspects and cleans the flange, installs a new seal, sets and levels the toilet and connects a new supply line. A leak check follows.

Installation follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), which sets clearances and flush volume limits.

Septic and well homes

Special cases outside city sewer

Houses on septic systems are more sensitive to what goes down the toilet. Wipes, even those labeled flushable, do not break down in a tank. A slow toilet in a septic home should be checked before it becomes a backup. If the toilet is flushed and the drain is the problem, drain clearing is the next step.

On a well, a running toilet wastes water the pump had to lift and can make the pump cycle more often. Fixing the flapper protects the pump as well as the bill.

Running toilets

A toilet that runs, leaks or will not flush

A running toilet usually has a worn flapper or a fill valve that no longer shuts off. Both are inexpensive parts, and a toilet that runs all night adds to the water bill. A leak at the base points to a wax ring.

A toilet that will not flush needs a look at the trap and the line behind it. If tub and sink drains also gurgle, the clog is deeper than the toilet.
